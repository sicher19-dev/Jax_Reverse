How to read the grades: every hitter you faced gets one. CLEAN = you executed the plan. NOISE = good pitch, he beat it (or a loud out) — tip the cap. SOFT = cheap luck against you. FIXABLE = on you, and correctable. This was the best process grade of the three starts we've reviewed this way — three fixables in eighteen hitters, and the count control that carried Chicago came all the way back.
First — What Carried It

Strike one came back. That was the whole outing.

First-pitch strikes: 72% — up from 53% in New York and past the 62% from Chicago. That's why 66 pitches bought five innings: Baldwin, Harris, Yastrzemski and Dubón all made outs in one or two pitches at least once, the contact hitters got exactly the quick at-bats the plan asked for, and Acuña went 0-for-2 with no walk — never behind him except one 1-0 sinker, never anything up. The Soto plan, run again.

Now the honest half. The outs were loud. Six of fourteen balls in play were hit 95 or harder, and four rockets — Acuña 109, Olson 104, Harris 103, Dubón 100 — found gloves. The line was cleaner than the contact. Two reasons: the changeup got zero swing-and-misses (half of them were still above the knees), and the curve — the best pitch you have right now — was thrown four times all night. The put-away stuff mostly stayed in the pocket, so it was a contact-management win, not a strikeout night. Bank the count control; next start the finishing pitches come out.

The one run: a 3-1 sinker to Baldwin, knee-high on the inner half — hit 103. The location wasn't the problem; the count was. From behind, the sinker is the pitch they're waiting for, and he's a 52%-hard-swing hitter. Same lesson as Colson's double in August, one count later.

The Game Plan — What Held, What Slipped
HELD
Acuña only from ahead — corners and count

Eight pitches, 0-for-2, no walk, nothing up in the zone. First at-bat ran 0-2 exactly as drawn and ended on a sweeper away (a 109-mph rocket, caught). One 1-0 sinker snuck in — the only behind-count pitch he saw — and the next at-bat ended in a pop fly. The toughest out on the card, contained.

HELD
Strike one to everybody

72% first-pitch strikes — the best of the three reviewed starts. Only 7 of 18 hitters got to two strikes, but that's because the contact hitters were putting strike one and two in play, which is what 66 pitches for five innings looks like. Efficiency held; the strikeouts came later or not at all.

HALF HELD
The changeup below the knees

Ten thrown: five below the knees, three at the thigh, two higher. Zero swing-and-misses. The one hit hard (Olson, 104 mph) was the one at 2.55 feet. The two low ones to Yastrzemski and Harris got soft fly balls. Same story as New York, lower stakes.

HALF HELD
Dubón: two pitches and out

First at-bat: nine pitches, a changeup he never needed to see, and a 65-mph bloop that fell. Second at-bat: two pitches, line drive, caught. The card was right both times — the second one just followed it.

HALF HELD
Hunt the strikeouts in the bottom half

Albies: the surprise sinker froze him for strike three. Riley: four-seam above the zone, swung under it. Those are the two strikeouts, and both came from the plan. But Murphy got a three-pitch pop fly instead of a hunt, and the curve was thrown four times all night — the pitch with a 64% miss rate coming in stayed in the pocket. Two strikeouts is the cost.

HALF HELD
Surprise sinker once per hitter · sweeper out of the middle

Albies saw the two-strike sinker in two different at-bats — froze on the first, put the second in play. Once per hitter means once per game. Three sweepers caught the middle of the plate; none were hit, all were reminders.

What We're Watching Next Start

The short list

✓ Count control is back. 72% first-pitch strikes, 66 pitches for five innings, Acuña contained. This is the standard now — keep it.
✗ The behind-count sinker. Baldwin's homer was 3-1. When you're behind, the sinker is the pitch they're sitting on — the changeup or sweeper is the pitch there, or better, don't get there.
✗ Use the curve. Four all night for the pitch with the best miss rate in the bag. It finished nobody because it was never asked to.
✗ Changeup height, still. Half of them lived below the knees; the half that didn't got hit 104 or got lucky. No misses on it two starts running.
✓ Movement holds. Sinker and changeup still running together, fastball 96 in the first. Question stays closed.
THE SHORT VERSION

The way we'd read it together: that was the count-control start we talked about after New York. 72% first-pitch strikes, five innings on 66 pitches, and Acuña went quietly twice — nothing up, nothing from behind, the Soto plan working on the best hitter in their yard. The one run was a 3-1 sinker; from behind, that's the pitch they're waiting for. That's the whole fix.

The honest part is the contact. Four balls hit 100-plus went right at gloves — Acuña, Olson, Harris, Dubón — so the line was a little cleaner than the night. The reason is the finishing pitches stayed in the pocket: the changeup still hasn't gotten a swing-and-miss in two starts, mostly because half of them are still at the thigh, and the curve — your best pitch right now — came out four times. That's not a problem to fix so much as a pitch to start trusting.

So the way we see it: the foundation is back. Strike one is the standard now, the movement's home, the four-seam above the zone keeps finishing people. Next one, we'd like the curve and the buried changeup doing what the count control is setting up. And whenever the grip photo's handy — no rush.
