The Game Plan — What Held, What Slipped
SLIPPED — BUT THE PLAN WORKS
Soto: nothing over the middle, ever

Two of his five pitches caught the middle of the plate; the second — a changeup — left at 105 mph. But look at his second at-bat: curve below the zone (he swung over it), sinker on the corner, curve down — easy fly ball. One at-bat broke the rule, one at-bat ran the plan perfectly. Keep both in the memory bank.

SLIPPED
The changeup has to live below the knees

Four balls hit 100+ tonight — Soto, Young, Lindor, Baty — all on changeups that sat thigh-high. The only strikeout came on a changeup at the shins, and both changeup swing-and-misses were below the knees. Same pitch, two stories: down there it strikes people out; up at the thigh it gets hit hard.

HALF HELD
Semien: sweeper away, all day

He saw sweepers on 6 of 10 pitches — the plan was followed — until the finish: the two-strike double came on a sinker that rode up (hit 104, the biggest damage of the night), and ball four was a sweeper a foot outside. Right plan, finishing pitches missed.

HALF HELD
Bichette & Benge: hard stuff only

Bichette: sinkers and a sweeper — held. Benge: mostly hard — held — but the 7-pitch walk on a full-count nibble is the same pattern as the Seattle game in July: trying to be too fine instead of making a rookie earn it.

SLIPPED — THE BIG ONE
Getting ahead, getting to two strikes

In Chicago you took 16 of 21 hitters to two strikes and walked nobody. Tonight: 7 of 17, and first-pitch strikes fell from 62% to 53%. Only five swing-and-misses all night — not because the pitches were worse, but because you were rarely ahead enough to use the put-away stuff. This one number is the whole gap between the two starts.

SLIPPED
The surprise sinker: once per hitter

The two-strike sinker works on lefties because they forget about it — they're 1-for-15 swinging at it this season. Tonight it went to lefties four times at two strikes, three of them to Lindor in one at-bat. Three in a row isn't a surprise anymore. Back to once per hitter, with the changeup and curve doing the finishing.

What We're Watching Next Start

The short list

✓ Your movement question: answered. The run is back — sinker deeper than August, changeup matching it. The grip conversation can happen whenever you want; there's no alarm behind it now.
✗ Changeup height — below the knees or don't throw it. Four hits at 100+ came at thigh height; every swing-and-miss came at the shins.
✗ Strike one. Getting to two strikes carried Chicago (16 of 21) and never happened tonight (7 of 17). It's the one lever that fixes everything else.
✓ The Soto plan is proven. Curve under the zone, corners, easy out. It's in the bank — it just has to happen every at-bat.
✗ The two walks were both deep-count nibbles. Chicago's zero walks came from attacking early, not from being careful.
THE SHORT VERSION

Honest sheet tonight, so here's how we'd read it together: the thing you were actually worried about is the thing that came back perfect. The sinker ran deeper than it did in August, the changeup matched it, and the fastball was 95–96 in the first. Whatever Durham looked like, it stayed in Durham — and that was the one question only a real game could answer.

The line is what rust looks like when the pitches are fine: the misses were height, not movement. The changeups that got hit were all at the thigh; the one you buried is still striking people out. The ball over the middle to Soto and the sinker to Semien were pitch-choice slips — and Soto's second at-bat proved the plan itself is right, because you ran it perfectly and got an easy out. And the getting-ahead part — the thing that carried Chicago — just never got rolling. That's the most fixable thing in baseball.

So the way we see it: bad line, good bones. The movement's home, the four-seam up top still works, the Soto plan is banked. Next one starts with strike one — and whenever the grip photo's handy, we'll pick that conversation back up. No rush now that the run's back.
